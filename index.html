June 2026: Charleston Police Warn of Fake Events Sold Online

On June 23, 2026, the Charleston Police Department issued a public warning about a rise in fraudulent events advertised online and on social media. According to the department, scammers are building fake event pages or promoting events that don't exist. Victims often pay through unofficial websites or peer-to-peer apps, then find out the event isn't real or their tickets won't scan.

Police told News 2 (WCBD) the scams are getting more sophisticated, with professional-looking websites and social ads built to look like real promotions. The department noted that scammers take advantage of Charleston's busy calendar of festivals and events. Few markets fit that description better than bachelorette weekends, where groups book months ahead, from out of town, and often can't check a venue in person.

CPD's Recommended Precautions
  • City-sponsored events carry a Special Events Committee permit number (format: SEC2026-#####)
  • Buy only from the organizer's official website or an authorized ticket vendor
  • Confirm the event appears on the organizer's official website or social pages
  • Be wary of prices far below market value
  • Avoid paying with Venmo, Cash App, or other apps with little buyer protection
  • Research the event and website before entering payment information

Private ticketed shows don't need a city special-events permit, so a missing SEC number alone doesn't prove a male revue is fake. But the rest of CPD's advice applies directly: verify the venue yourself, pay with a credit card, and treat a slick website as proof of marketing, not proof of a show.

Notice for Bachelorette Parties

A "touring" revue that plays the same bar every weekend isn't touring

A touring production travels. A show that runs Thursday through Sunday at the same downtown bar all year is a resident operation. In the City of Charleston, adult entertainment of that kind is only allowed in industrial-zoned locations, and downtown bar districts aren't zoned for it.

Before you buy from Eventbrite, a social ad, or a brand website, call the listed venue directly (using a number you look up yourself) and ask whether they are hosting that event on your date.

The Aura Precedent: The City Has Already Shut Down a Downtown Club Over Male Strip Shows

Enforcement History · June 2024

On June 14, 2024, the City of Charleston's License Committee revoked the business license of Ann Street Brand LLC, operator of Aura Latin Club at 28 Ann Street (formerly Deco). Per News 2's reporting on the committee's written decision, the club was found to be running male strip shows on a regular basis, which its zoning did not allow. Assaults, underage drinking, and other incidents were also cited.

The attorney who represented the city told News 2 that these operations are only allowed in light industrial or heavy industrial zones, and that the strip shows were added to the reasons for the revocation. Mayor William Cogswell's statement said the club violated city law by hosting and selling tickets to male strip shows.

The club's operator disputed the decision, saying the city gave no warning and that similar shows had run at other locations in the area for years. That point matters for consumers: the fact that shows are happening doesn't mean they're allowed, and a venue that loses its license can close with no notice to people holding tickets.

How Out-of-State, Multi-City Revue Brands Market to Bachelorette Destinations

Search "male revue" plus almost any bachelorette city and you'll see the same handful of national brands. Some are long-running companies with real shows. The business model, though, creates problems that local groups should understand before buying.

One well-known New York–based brand, for example, says on its locations page that it runs shows Thursday through Sunday in more than 40 cities, with Southeast listings that include Charleston, Myrtle Beach, Savannah, Charlotte, and Key West. Many of those listings are hosted inside existing bars, lounges, or clubs rather than dedicated theaters. A company can't physically staff 40 cities with a fixed cast, so the performers, hosting venue, and even the date can change.

PATTERN 01
One brand, dozens of "local" pages

City-specific pages and domains that each claim to be the "#1" or "longest-running" show in that city, with nearly identical copy swapped between cities.

PATTERN 02
Rented rooms, not venues

The "club" is a back room or a booked night at an existing bar. If the bar drops the event or the city steps in, the show moves or disappears.

PATTERN 03
The venue that never heard of it

Consumer reviews of multi-city revue sellers describe groups getting a venue-change text days before the show, calling the new venue, and learning it knew nothing about the event.

PATTERN 04
"Rebook in another city"

Reviews also describe last-minute cancellations where the refund offer was a ticket to a show in a different city, which is useless to a group with travel and lodging already booked.

Why This Matters in Charleston

A national brand listing Charleston as one of its cities doesn't tell you where the show will actually happen, whether that location is allowed to host it, or what happens to your money if the venue backs out. Ask for a specific street address, call that venue yourself, and pay with a credit card so you can dispute the charge.

Don't Choose Entertainers from Online Galleries

Groups often spend hours comparing performer galleries. That time is usually wasted. A small number of performers serve the Charleston market, often under several brand names, and choosing a face from a gallery doesn't guarantee who shows up.

Many gallery and stage photos aren't taken in Charleston at all. They come from national productions, stock libraries, or other markets. A quick reverse image search (Google Lens or TinEye) will often show the same photo on sites for several other cities.

Large theater shots with professional lighting and packed crowds usually come from national productions, not a Charleston back room. If a seller can't show recent photos from an actual Charleston event, factor that into your decision.

Charleston Zoning Keeps Adult Entertainment Out of Downtown

Within Charleston city limits, adult uses are allowed only in Light Industrial (LI) and Heavy Industrial (HI) zones, and even there they face distance buffers from schools, churches, parks, and residential areas. That's the standard the city's attorney described when the Aura license was revoked. Unincorporated areas fall under Charleston County's separate regulations (Section 6.4.18, Sexually Oriented Businesses).

Why "Private Event" Framing Doesn't Settle It

Calling a ticketed show a "private party" or holding it behind a curtain doesn't change the zoning of the building. In the Aura case, the city looked at what was actually happening on site, not how the event was marketed.

What's at Stake for Venues (and Your Booking)

A bar that hosts shows its zoning doesn't allow is risking its city business license, and potentially its state alcohol permits. As Aura showed, the city can revoke a license and close the doors. When that happens, ticket holders are the ones left scrambling.

Possible Consequences for Venues
  • Revocation of the city business license
  • Scrutiny of state alcohol permits
  • Fines and legal costs
  • Sudden closure, with no show for ticket holders

Drink minimums or bottle packages bundled into tickets aren't proof of wrongdoing on their own, but at a downtown bar they're a sign the "show" is really a bar night built around a revue. Ask what you're actually paying for and whether it's refundable.

Why Some Shows Keep Running

Zoning and license problems often surface only after complaints, police calls, or a license review. In the Aura case, the strip shows came to the city's attention while it was already acting on other problems at the club.

Tickets are sold by an out-of-state website or a ticketing platform, not the bar. The seller may never be in Charleston, and the bar may only be renting out a room for the night.

No enforcement so far doesn't mean a show is allowed. Media attention, complaints, or a license renewal can end a recurring show without warning to ticket holders.

Last-Minute Venue Changes Are a Red Flag

A common complaint about multi-city revue sellers is the venue-change message a few days before the show. Sometimes the new location doesn't know about the event. Sometimes the show is cancelled outright.

Bait-and-Switch Warning

Ask for a refund in writing if a change moves your group to:

  • A hookah lounge or downtown bar
  • Any address on King Street, East Bay Street, or Ann Street
  • A venue that won't confirm the event when you call it directly
  • A "show" in another city
Steps to Request a Refund
01
Save everything: the original listing, confirmation, venue address, and every message about the change.
02
Call the new venue yourself and note whether they confirm the event.
03
Request a full refund in writing from the seller, citing the change from the advertised venue.
04
Contact the ticket platform (e.g., Eventbrite) and use its refund or dispute process.
05
Dispute the charge with your card issuer if the refund is refused ("not as described" or "not received").
06
Report it to the SC Department of Consumer Affairs, the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and Charleston Police's online reporting site.

Questions to Ask Before You Pay

What is the exact street address?
Not "downtown Charleston" or a neighborhood name.
Can I call the venue to confirm the date?
Look up the venue's number yourself rather than using one the seller gives you.
Who is actually selling the tickets, and where is that company based?
An out-of-state seller with no local contact makes refunds harder.
What's the refund policy if the venue changes or the show is cancelled?
Get it in writing. "Rebook in another city" is not a refund.
Can I pay by credit card?
CPD specifically warns against Venmo, Cash App, and similar apps.

Warning Signs on Booking Websites

  • No street address, or an address that belongs to an unrelated business.
  • Copy-and-paste city pages where only the city name changes.
  • "Every Thursday–Sunday" in dozens of cities with no named venue for yours.
  • Venues listed without their knowledge. Call and ask.
  • Gallery or stage photos that turn up on other cities' sites in a reverse image search.
  • Payment requested by peer-to-peer app or before the venue is disclosed.
  • A brand-new domain. A WHOIS lookup shows when a site was registered.
